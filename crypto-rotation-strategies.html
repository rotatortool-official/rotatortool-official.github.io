Crypto rotation strategies: methods, evidence and risks

Most rotation guides describe a strategy and stop there. This one shows what happened when we tested six rotation rules on the same two years of Binance coins, including the ones that lost, and what fees, turnover and false signals do to the rest.

By Daniel, who builds Rotator · Published 3 Nov 2026 · Last checked 3 Nov 2026. New to the idea? Start with what capital rotation is.

How we tested

Every day from 27 Jun 2024 to 6 Aug 2026 (771 days), each rule picked five pairs: a coin to leave and a coin to move into. The coin you move into must pass the same eligibility check the board uses. A pair wins when the coin you moved into did better than the coin you left over the next 7, 14 or 30 days. Two coins picked at random win about 50% of the time, so that is the line to beat.

The rules and the pass bar were written down and committed before the first run (23 Sep 2026), so we could not pick the winner after seeing the results. To pass, a rule had to win at least 52% of pairs and gain on average in both halves of the history separately. A rule that only worked in one half is treated as luck.

What each method teaches

Mean reversion: buying the laggards

This is the classic rotation: sell what ran, buy what lagged. In our data it lost, whether we ranked coins by score or kept to the 20 biggest coins. Weak coins tended to stay weak. This was Rotator's own first published call, which is why the board no longer makes it.

Relative strength: buying the leaders

The mirror image, moving into the coins that have been strongest, did better than random but not by enough, and not steadily in both halves. Among the 20 biggest coins it was the only rule that passed, at 7 days, winning 53.3% of pairs with an average edge of about +0.35% per pair. That is small. It earned a live test, not a recommendation.

Price-only laggards

Moving from the biggest 30-day gainers into the biggest 30-day losers looked good overall (55.3% at 30 days), but all of that came from the second year: in the first year it was 50.0%, a coin flip. One good year is not a strategy.

Bitcoin → Ethereum → altcoins

The famous sequence is a hypothesis about whole market cycles, and two years of data contain at most one. We have not tested it as a timing rule, and we don't think anyone can test it properly yet. It is still a useful way to describe what has happened.

Sector rotation

The board's category tabs (L1, DeFi, L2, Meme, AI, Gaming) let you see which group leads. We have not tested sector rotation as a rule, so we make no claim either way.

Still being tested: big caps, into the leaders

The one rule that passed runs live every day since 23 Sep 2026, graded at 7 days. Over the last 30 graded days (to 2 Nov 2026), 54.7% of its pairs won. Before it started we wrote down the range the backtest expects for 30 days: 37.3% to 70.7%. The live result sits inside it, so it is not contradicted, and not proven: 30 days is far too short to tell skill from luck. The full one-year test of Rotator's calls ends in March 2027, and its results are on the track record.

Fees and turnover

Every rotation is two trades, a sell and a buy. At Binance's standard spot fee of 0.1% per trade, that is about 0.2% per rotation before any price slippage. The rule that passed had an average edge of about +0.35% per 7-day pair, so fees alone take more than half of it. Rotating every week means about 52 rotations a year, which is roughly 10% of the money moved in fees. Small coins are worse: wider spreads and thinner order books cost more than the fee.

False signals and risk

Even the rule that passed loses almost half of its pairs. Losing streaks of several weeks are normal at a 53% win rate, and they feel exactly like the rule has stopped working. Rotation also does not protect you from the market falling: if everything drops, a pair can "win" while both coins lose money. And coins carry risks that no ranking shows: delistings, large token unlocks, and supply still to come.

Limits

  • Two years of data, Binance coins only, about one market cycle.
  • The score was tuned on the same history, so every score-based rule here is flattered, not hurt. Splitting the history in two guards against choosing a rule by luck, not against that.
  • Win rates ignore fees and slippage (see above).
  • Pairs on nearby days overlap, so the samples are less independent than their size suggests.
