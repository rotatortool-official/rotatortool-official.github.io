What is crypto capital rotation?

Capital rotation is money leaving one part of the crypto market for another: out of Bitcoin into Ethereum, out of big coins into small ones, out of one sector into the next, or out of coins into stablecoins. This page explains the idea, what traders watch, and what our own tests found when we checked it against two years of data.

By Daniel, who builds Rotator · Published 3 Nov 2026 · Last checked 3 Nov 2026

The usual story: Bitcoin first, then the rest

The version you will read most often goes like this. New money comes into Bitcoin first, because it is the biggest and easiest coin to buy. When Bitcoin has run, traders look for the next thing and move into Ethereum. After that, money spreads to the other large coins, then to small and very small ones, and in the end it runs back to Bitcoin or to stablecoins when the market turns.

Treat it as a story, not a law. It describes some past bull markets well, but there have only been a few of them, so nobody can test it properly. Some recent cycles did not follow it at all, and many traders now argue the classic altcoin season has faded (Cointelegraph, citing CryptoQuant data).

Other kinds of rotation

Sector rotation is money moving between groups of coins: layer-1 chains, DeFi, AI coins, memes, gaming. Each group tends to have its turn when its story is popular. Risk rotation is money moving between risky coins and safe places, such as stablecoins or Bitcoin itself. Coin-to-coin rotation is the smallest kind: selling one coin that has run and buying another that has lagged.

What our own data says

Rotator started as a rotation tool. Its first published call was the coin-to-coin kind: leave the five coins with the highest score and move into the five with the lowest, on the idea that the leaders had run and the laggards would catch up. Before keeping it, we tested it on 771 days of Binance coins (27 Jun 2024 to 6 Aug 2026), about 3,800 pairs per time horizon. A pair counts as a win when the coin you moved into did better than the coin you left. Two coins picked at random win about 50% of the time.

It lost. Moving from the strong coins into the weak ones did worse than picking at random, at every horizon and in both halves of the history. Strong coins tended to stay strong for a while, and weak coins tended to keep lagging. That is why the board now shows scores as information and makes no rotation call. Other rules we tried, and the one that is still being tested, are on Crypto rotation strategies.

Limits of this test

It covers about two years, which is one market cycle at most, and only coins traded on Binance. The score was tuned on the same history, which flatters the score rather than hurts it, so the real result is unlikely to be better than this. It ignores trading fees, which would make every rotation look worse. A live test of the published calls started on 11 Sep 2026 and is graded in public on the track record. Its verdict is due in March 2027.

So how should you use rotation?

As a way to look at the market, not as a signal. Seeing that money has moved into one sector, or that a coin you hold has fallen behind Bitcoin, is useful context. Acting on it automatically has not paid off in our data. If you hold coins, the more useful questions are often simpler: is a big unlock coming, is the coin about to be delisted, has it fallen through your own price?
